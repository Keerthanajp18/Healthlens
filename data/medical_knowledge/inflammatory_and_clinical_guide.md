# Clinical Guide to Inflammatory Markers and Lab Interpretation

## 1. C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and High-Sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP)
C-Reactive Protein (CRP) is a pentameric acute-phase reactant protein synthesized by hepatocytes in the liver under the stimulation of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and other pro-inflammatory cytokines.
- **What it measures**: Measures systemic inflammatory activity in the body.
- **Reference Ranges**:
  - Standard CRP: < 3.0 mg/L (or < 0.3 mg/dL) indicates normal levels. Values > 10.0 mg/L indicate clinically significant acute bacterial infections, active autoimmune flares, or major tissue trauma.
  - High-Sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) for Cardiovascular Risk:
    - Low risk: < 1.0 mg/L
    - Average risk: 1.0 - 3.0 mg/L
    - High risk: > 3.0 mg/L
- **Clinical Significance**:
  - CRP rises rapidly within 6 to 8 hours of inflammatory onset and peaks at 48 hours.
  - Markedly high levels (> 50 - 100 mg/L) strongly suggest bacterial infection (such as bacterial pneumonia or sepsis), active rheumatoid arthritis flare, giant cell arteritis, or pancreatitis.

## 2. Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)
The Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate, commonly referred to as the sed rate, measures the rate at which red blood cells in anticoagulated whole blood settle to the bottom of a vertical standardized tube over one hour.
- **What it measures**: An indirect kinetic measure of circulating acute-phase proteins, particularly fibrinogen and immunoglobulins, which neutralize negative surface charges (zeta potential) on red cells, promoting aggregation (rouleaux formation).
- **Reference Ranges**:
  - Men (< 50 years): 0 - 15 mm/hr
  - Men (>= 50 years): 0 - 20 mm/hr
  - Women (< 50 years): 0 - 20 mm/hr
  - Women (>= 50 years): 0 - 30 mm/hr
- **Clinical Significance**:
  - Extreme elevation (> 100 mm/hr) has high predictive value for specific conditions including temporal arteritis / giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, multiple myeloma, metastatic malignancy, or osteomyelitis.

## 3. Serum Ferritin
Ferritin is an intracellular iron storage protein complex found in all tissues, but highly concentrated in liver hepatocytes, spleen, and bone marrow.
- **What it measures**: Quantifies total body iron stores and also serves as an acute-phase reactant.
- **Reference Ranges**:
  - Adult Males: 24 - 336 ng/mL (or mcg/L)
  - Adult Females: 11 - 307 ng/mL (pre-menopausal often lower, 15 - 150 ng/mL)
- **Clinical Significance**:
  - Low Ferritin (< 15 - 30 ng/mL): The single most specific diagnostic laboratory finding for absolute iron deficiency anemia.
  - High Ferritin (> 500 - 1000 ng/mL): Indicates iron overload (hereditary hemochromatosis, repeated transfusions) or severe hyperferritinemic systemic inflammation (Still's disease, HLH, severe COVID-19/sepsis, chronic liver disease).
